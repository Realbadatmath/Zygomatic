The yard at Kattenburg woke before the city did. Long before the bells, before the
first cart came down over the bridge, the watchman walked the length of the slip and
counted what the night had left him: two hulls on the stocks, a stack of oak he did
not trust, and a tide already turning against the gate. He wrote none of it down. The
writing was my work, and I came at six with cold hands and a dry pen.

I had been told that a ledger is a record. It is not. A ledger is an argument, made
twice a day, between what a yard has and what a yard has promised, and the man who
keeps it learns very quickly which of the two the owners would rather read. I kept it
honestly for eleven years. I am not certain that honesty served anybody but me.

Cornelis came out of the shed at half past six with his mallet under his arm and his
coat still open, and he stood where he always stood, at the stern of the larger hull,
looking up at her as though she had said something to him in the night. He was sixty
that winter, or near enough that nobody argued. His hands were ruined. He could still
set a plank truer by eye than the young men could with a rule, and he knew it, and
that knowledge was the only vanity he allowed himself.

Joost was the other kind of shipwright. He measured everything, twice, and wrote the
numbers on the wood in chalk so that anyone who doubted him could go and look. The two
of them had worked the same yard since before I came and they had never once agreed
about anything that mattered. When the owners wanted a decision they asked Cornelis.
When they wanted a defence they asked Joost. The ship got built either way.

That autumn the orders came in faster than the timber. The agents in the south had
promised delivery in the spring and the spring was no longer a distant thing; you
could feel it in how the men worked, in the particular silence of a yard that is
behind. Witsen came down from the office on the fourteenth to tell us what we already
knew. He read his figures to the four of us in the cold, and he did not look up from
the page while he read them, which I took at the time for cowardice and understand now
as something nearer to shame.

We were short. Not by a little. The pitch had not arrived, the second consignment of
oak had been sold out from under us by a broker in Hoorn, and the ropewalk wanted
payment before it would spin another fathom. I remember the numbers because I had
written most of them, and I remember thinking that a man who writes a number down has
already half decided to believe it.

Cornelis said we would build her anyway. Joost said that we would build her badly, and
that a ship built badly comes back, and that when she comes back she does not come back
to the office. They said these things quietly, because the men were listening, and
because neither of them wanted to be the one who said the other word.

I have thought about that morning more than any other. Not because anything was decided
there, but because it is the last morning I can remember in which the yard still
believed it had a choice. Everything after it followed, and followed reasonably, and
each step was defensible on its own, and at the end of it a ship went out over the bar
in February with green timber in her and men aboard who trusted us.

The ledger closed that year at a profit. I wrote the figure myself, in my own hand,
and I have never been able to look at it since without hearing the tide come up under
the gate.
